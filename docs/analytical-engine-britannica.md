# The Analytical Engine — Foundation Notes

Distilled 2026-11-18 from Britannica: "Analytical Engine" by Michael R.
Swaine & Paul A. Freiberger (revised 9 Oct 2025), plus its companion Ada
Lovelace article.
Source: https://www.britannica.com/technology/Analytical-Engine

## First computer, designed 1833+, never completed

While building the government-commissioned Difference Engine, Charles
Babbage imagined generalizing it. When Difference Engine funding ran out
in 1833, he had conceived the Analytical Engine: a general-purpose, fully
program-controlled, automatic mechanical digital computer, able to perform
any calculation set before it. No prior evidence exists that anyone
conceived such a device before. He worked on it until his death in 1871;
a portion containing the mill and a printing mechanism was completed in
1910.

## The four components — the organs of every computer since

1. **Mill** — the calculating unit, analogous to a modern CPU
2. **Store** — data held prior to processing, analogous to memory and
   storage (planned: 1,000 50-digit numbers, larger than any real
   computer before 1960)
3. **Reader** — input via punched cards, using the Jacquard loom's
   card-reading technology
4. **Printer** — output, with automated printed tables of numbers

Steam-driven, run by one attendant.

## Why it was a computer and not a calculator

- **Instruction cards** (also Jacquard's idea): swapping cards changes
  what the machine does — the most revolutionary feature. Before it,
  every mechanical aid to calculation was (at best) a glorified calculator.
- **Conditional branching** (conditional control transfer): jump to a
  different instruction depending on the value of some data — a feature
  missing in many early 20th-century computers.

## Ada Lovelace, 1843 — the first program

Translating and annotating Menabrea's article on the Engine, Lovelace
described how it could be programmed to compute Bernoulli numbers — the
first computer program, and her claim to "first computer programmer." Her
line: "The Analytical Engine weaves algebraic patterns, just as the
Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves." She saw it manipulating symbols
beyond numbers (musical notes, letters), while also stating it "has no
pretensions whatever to originate anything" (later Turing's "Lady
Lovelace's Objection").

## Why it failed to be built

Implementation judged infeasible with then-current technology, and the
British government — burned by the unfinished Difference Engine and aware
Babbage preferred innovation over producing tables — declined further
funding.
